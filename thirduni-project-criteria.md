## Who judges your project

The panel is made up of judges from the **U.S. Embassy** and one guest judge from a major Algerian company, to be announced before judging week.

**No member of the Thirduni team scores any project.** Instructors, mentors and programme staff take no part in scoring and have no vote. Our role is limited to checking that submissions are complete and eligible, and passing them to the panel. Judges score independently and do not see each other's scores while scoring.

If a judge has a personal or professional connection to a team, they declare it and do not score that team. That team's score is the average of the remaining judges.

## What you must submit

Eight items. A submission missing any of items 1 to 6 is marked incomplete and cannot be scored, so check this list before the deadline.

**1\. Project name and one-line summary** One sentence, maximum 20 words. What it does, for whom.

**2\. Project description** Maximum 150 words. The problem, your solution, and who it is for. Write it for someone who has never met you.

**3\. Live demo link** A URL a judge can open in a browser and use without installing anything. If your project cannot be deployed (a mobile app, a hardware project, something that needs credentials), say so in your description and make sure your demo video carries the weight instead.

**4\. Code repository** A public GitHub link containing your code and a README. The README must cover what the project is, how to run it locally, and who built which part. A repo with no README loses points under technical execution.

**5\. Demo video** Two to four minutes, uploaded to YouTube or Google Drive. Unlisted is fine, private is not. Judges must be able to open it without requesting access, and this is the single most common reason submissions fail.

Show the product working. Screen recording with your voice over it is enough. You do not need editing, music or a title sequence. Do not spend four minutes on slides and thirty seconds on the product.

**6\. Team members and roles** Every member's full name, plus one line each on what they actually did. Be honest. "Designed the database and wrote the auth flow" is useful. "Helped with everything" is not.

**7\. Tech stack** A list of the languages, frameworks, services and AI tools you used. Using AI tools heavily is expected on this programme and costs you nothing. Hiding it does.

**8\. What happens next** Maximum 100 words. What you would build in the next three months if you kept going. Teams who clearly intend to continue score better under impact and viability.

Optional, and it helps: a pitch deck as a PDF, maximum eight slides.

## How you are scored

Five criteria. Each judge scores each criterion from 1 to 5, and each criterion is worth 20 points, so your total is out of 100\. Nothing is weighted more heavily than anything else.

The scale: **1** far below expectations, **2** below, **3** meets expectations, **4** above, **5** exceptional.

Your final score is the average across all judges.

### 1\. Problem and insight (20 points)

Is the problem real, specific and worth solving, and do you understand the people who have it?

Scores well: a narrow problem, described precisely, with evidence you talked to somebody who actually has it.

Scores badly: a problem so broad it could describe anything, or a solution that was obviously chosen first and had a problem attached to it afterwards.

### 2\. Working product (20 points)

Can a judge open your project and use it? Does the main thing it promises actually work?

Scores well: the core feature works end to end, even if nothing else does.

Scores badly: a polished interface where nothing is connected, or a demo that only works if you already know which buttons to avoid.

One finished feature beats five half-built ones. Every time.

### 3\. Technical execution (20 points)

How is it built? Could another developer pick up your repo and understand it?

Scores well: a clear README, sensible structure, code someone else could read, honest handling of the parts you did not finish.

Scores badly: no README, secrets committed to the repo, or a codebase nobody on the team can explain.

### 4\. Impact and viability (20 points)

Who does this help, how many of them are there, and what happens to the project after October?

Scores well: a specific group of people who would use this, a realistic sense of what it would take to reach them, and a reason to believe you will keep going.

Scores badly: large unsupported numbers, or a project that clearly ends the moment it is submitted.

### 5\. Story and demo (20 points)

Can you explain what you built, why it matters and how it works, clearly, in the time you have?

Scores well: a demo video that opens with the product, explains the problem in under thirty seconds, and shows real usage.

Scores badly: four minutes of context before anything is shown, or a video where the screen is unreadable.

## Tie-breaks

If two teams finish on the same total, the panel ranks them on **working product** first, then **problem and insight**. If they are still level, the panel discusses and decides together.

---

## Rules

- Your project must be built by your team during the programme. Earlier work can be used as a starting point only if you say so clearly in your description and submission.  
- Open source libraries, templates, APIs and AI tools are all allowed and encouraged. Submitting someone else's project as your own is not.  
- One submission per team.  
- Everything you submit must be in English or French.  
- Teams must be registered on the platform before 31 October to submit.

---

## Common ways teams lose points for no reason

These have nothing to do with how good your project is, and they happen every single time:

- A demo video set to private, so judges cannot open it  
- A repo set to private, same problem  
- A deployed link that was working last week and is down on 1 November  
- No README  
- A description that explains the technology and never says what the product does  
- Submitting at 23:55 on 31 October and discovering an upload fails

Check all your links in a private browser window, logged out, on 30 October.
